SLS Annual Assembly 2026 · Concept note

Built by the room

The Assembly is the one day of the year the whole society is in a single place. That makes it the only occasion on which SLS can properly do what it cannot do anywhere else: name the partners, Council members, Chapter teams and contributors who carried the year, in front of everyone whose year they carried. Everything else the day does follows from that.

01Objective

Recognise. Show. Launch.

To close the SLS year by recognising the people who carried it — partners, Council members, Chapter teams and key contributors — in front of the full membership; to show what their work produced; and to open 2027 with members committed to it before they leave the room.

Three verbs, in that order. Recognition comes first because it is the only thing on the agenda that cannot be done at any other SLS event, and because a society that shows its impact without naming who delivered it has told only half the story.

  • Every contributor group named publicly, each with one specific line on what they did and what it produced — never a category thanked in the abstract.
  • ≥85% of confirmed guests actually attend. In 2024, 320 of 468 registrants came — 68%.
  • Overall satisfaction ≥85% (2024: 76.8%) and agenda flow ≥85% (2024: 69.8%, the lowest-scoring item on the survey).
  • ≥60 members leave with a named 2027 commitment — a visit hosted, a circle chaired, a chapter opened, a member mentored.
  • ≥3 introductions logged per member on the network graph.
02Theme

Built by the room

Everything on the screen tonight traces back to someone sitting in this room. Next year’s will too.

The line does three jobs at once. It makes the recognition argument without a speech — every milestone shown has a person attached, and they are present. It reads backwards as a statement about the year that closed and forwards as an instruction for the one opening. And it survives compression: it works as a stage graphic, an invitation subject line, and four words on a badge.

It also sets an editorial test for the agenda, which is where themes usually stop being useful and this one keeps earning: if a segment does not connect to somebody in the room, it does not belong on the programme.

  • Everything here has a name on it — more literal, excellent as the headline over the recognition wall, weaker as a stage line and too long for an invitation.
  • The year, in names — quieter and more formal. Better if the Council wants the tone closer to an honours evening than a community night.

One dependency to book in week one: the Arabic line. It has to carry the meaning rather than translate the words, and the theme drives the invitation, the wall and the stage design — so it cannot be a late decision. Brief comms before anything else is commissioned.

03Format

One day, one room, five movements

RegisterFormal enough to honour people properly, informal enough that they talk to each other afterwards
LanguageBilingual throughout; simultaneous translation in scope

The rule that protects the agenda: if a segment would work at a Chapter event, it does not belong on the Assembly.

The Assembly’s only structural advantage over everything else SLS runs is that everyone is there at once. So it should spend itself on the four things that need full scale to mean anything: recognition in front of the whole society, the complete network in one view, cross-chapter and cross-sector mixing that a chapter meeting structurally cannot produce, and decisions that are only legitimate when the whole membership is present.

Compared with a standard convening this trades a second keynote for the recognition moments — the right trade for a year-closing assembly, and one worth stating out loud so it is not quietly reversed during planning.

05Recognition approach

Three volumes, because sixty names is not a ceremony

Four groups deserve recognition — partners, Council, Chapter teams, contributors — and together they are likely sixty-plus names. Sixty walks to a stage is forty minutes of applause fatigue, and it lands precisely on the thing the 2024 survey scored lowest. Recognition has to be designed at three different volumes.

The Wall of the Year

Tier 1 · everyone · on arrival

A physical installation at the entrance carrying every partner, Council member, Chapter team and contributor, grouped by what they did rather than by rank. Sixty names honoured in the time it takes to walk in — and, done properly, the most photographed object of the night. Guests find their own name before anyone says a word from a stage.

In context, on stage

Tier 2 · four groups · ninety seconds each

Each group is recognised inside the segment it belongs to, not queued in an awards block: partners as the impact segment opens, because the impact is what they enabled; Chapter teams during the Chapters segment, called up as teams; the Council at the roadmap handover, which is their work; contributors named on screen through dinner. Every named recognition carries one specific line — what they did and what it produced. “Thank you for your support” is worth nothing to an executive.

The peer-nominated honour

Tier 3 · one · 19:25

One honour, nominated by members four weeks out through the same flow that confirms their attendance. In a peer society a committee’s choice carries less weight than the room’s, and the nominations are useful twice: they decide the honour, and they hand the Track the specific evidence every Tier 2 line needs.

The letter home. A letter from SLS leadership to the board or CEO of each honoured contributor’s organisation. Recognition that reaches an executive’s employer outlives anything they can put on a shelf. It costs a signature and it is still remembered a year later.

The workstream this creates: evidence. Every named recognition needs one verified sentence, and those sentences do not exist yet. Chapters and the Council should be asked for them during the planning phase — not in the final week, which is how recognition segments end up generic.

06What makes it different

Six things a regular SLS event cannot do

  • The venue is the exhibition on arrival. The first hour is not queueing and coffee. The Wall, the live network graph and the year’s milestones are all walkable before anything starts, so arriving early is rewarded rather than endured.
  • The year is told by three members, not reviewed from a deck. Impact lands when it comes from the person it happened to. It also puts three more names in front of the room without adding a recognition segment.
  • Chapter teams go up as teams. Six people on stage together for ninety seconds beats six separate walks, and it is a truer picture of how chapter work actually gets done.
  • Ninety seconds with the lights down. The network graph replays from doors to close, showing every connection made during the day. One image, chosen deliberately, that people photograph.
  • The 2027 board. Before dinner, members commit publicly to one thing for next year — host a visit, chair a circle, open a chapter, mentor a member. Photographed on the night; it becomes the Council’s pipeline in January rather than a follow-up email nobody answers.
  • The takeaway is personal, not a gift bag. A card carrying your own node, your new connections and your commitment — which keeps the society in front of members through the eleven months when there is no Assembly.
