01Purpose

Show the year. Name the people who made it.

The Assembly is the one evening the whole society is in a single room. Two things need that room: the year’s achievements, set out properly, and the partners, Council members, Chapter teams and contributors who delivered them, recognized in front of everyone. Everything else is in service of those two.

The organizing line is built by the room — every achievement shown traces back to someone sitting in it, and next year’s will too. It doubles as the test for the agenda: if a segment doesn’t connect to somebody present, it comes out.

02What members leave with

The brief every segment is tested against

Before deciding what happens on stage, it is worth being exact about what we want in a member’s head on the drive home. This is the design brief; anything that doesn’t serve it comes off the agenda.

Thinking

  • I can name three things this society delivered in 2026.
  • What I contributed was seen — by name, in front of everyone.
  • I know what 2027 asks of me, and it is specific.

Feeling

  • Proud of the company I am in. Not flattered — proud.
  • Known. Someone in that room knows my name and what I worked on.
  • Impatient to start. Next year sounds worth being part of.

Doing, within a week

  • Repeats one SLS achievement to someone outside the room.
  • Says yes to one named thing for 2027.
03Key messages

Three things the evening has to land

  1. SLS is a working network, not a membership list. The proof is the year’s achievements, and every one of them has a member’s name on it.
  2. The society sees who does the work. Contribution is recognized publicly and specifically, not thanked in the abstract.
  3. 2027 asks something of you by name. Not “get involved” — a defined commitment, made before members leave.
04The content

The year’s achievements, at two depths

This is the part that has to be strongest, and the part that does not exist yet. No one has assembled what SLS delivered in 2026 in one place. Doing that is the first task, and everything on stage depends on it.

The gallery, at the entrance. The full year laid out to walk through during the first fifty minutes — every chapter, every initiative, every number. Members browse at their own pace and find their own work. This is where volume lives.

Three stories, on stage. Twenty-five minutes, three members telling what happened to them rather than a milestone review read from a deck. The test for choosing them:

  • A member did it, not the Foundation on their behalf.
  • The outcome fits in one sentence with a number in it.
  • It would not have happened without the society.

What we collect, and from whom. One request, one deadline, four categories — and the same request produces the recognition citations, so Chapters and the Council are asked once rather than twice:

  • What members built — initiatives and cross-sector projects launched this year.
  • What Chapters delivered — sessions held, members engaged, what came out of them.
  • How the society grew — new members from the Fellowship and 2030 Leaders cohorts, new chapters, international reach.
  • Where SLS showed up — partnerships, visits, contributions to national programs.

This is the critical path. Nothing on stage can be written until the achievements are in. The request goes to Chapter leads and the Council this month, due with the planning phase on November 19.

05Recognition

Three volumes, because sixty names is not a ceremony

Partners, Council, Chapter teams and contributors together are likely sixty-plus names. A queue of sixty walks to a stage would consume most of the evening and land on the item the 2024 survey scored lowest. So recognition runs at three volumes instead.

The Wall of the Year

Everyone · at the entrance, all evening

Every partner, Council member, Chapter team and contributor named, grouped by what they did rather than by rank, alongside the gallery. Sixty names honored in the time it takes to walk in, and members find their own before anyone speaks.

Two moments on stage

Four groups · 8:05 and 9:25

Partners and Chapter teams before the break; the Council and the honor after the keynote. Chapter teams go up as teams — six people together for ninety seconds beats six separate walks. Each named recognition carries one specific line: what they did and what it produced. “Thank you for your support” is worth nothing to an executive.

One honor, nominated by members

One · 9:25

Nominations collected on the RSVP form four weeks out. In a peer society the room’s choice carries more weight than a committee’s, and it costs one extra field on a form we are already sending.

The letter home. A letter from SLS leadership to the board or CEO of each honored contributor’s organization. Recognition that reaches an executive’s employer outlives anything they can put on a shelf. It costs a signature.

The keynote

No name yet — that is the Council’s to nominate. The criteria matter more than the shortlist: someone whose work members already follow, who can speak to what a network like SLS makes possible rather than to their own career, and who will be interviewed rather than deliver a speech. In 2024 members asked specifically for shorter, more inspiring speaking and for leaders sharing experience over general advice.

  • A Vision 2030 delivery leader — someone who has actually shipped a national program, interviewed by a member working on something adjacent. Closest to the theme; my recommendation.
  • A global voice on a subject the membership is already discussing. Strongest draw, weakest link to the society.
  • A member of the society whose year stands out. Cheapest and most on-theme, least external pull.

Booking at this level needs eight to ten weeks, so the shortlist should close with the planning phase on November 19.
